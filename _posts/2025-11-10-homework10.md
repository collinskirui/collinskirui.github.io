# Foundations of Probability — Homework

## 1. Interpretations of Probability

Probability can be understood in several ways depending on the context.  
Different interpretations emphasize different aspects — symmetry, long-run frequency, belief, or geometry — but all of them can be expressed within a single mathematical framework.

---

### 1.1 Classical Interpretation

The classical viewpoint applies when all outcomes are treated as equally possible.  
If an experiment has finitely many symmetric outcomes, the probability of an event \(A\) is

$$
P(A)=\frac{\text{number of favorable outcomes}}{\text{number of possible outcomes}}.
$$

This is natural for games involving dice, cards, or coins.  
Its limitation is that it only works when the sample space is finite and a symmetry argument actually exists.

---

### 1.2 Frequentist Interpretation

The frequentist picture defines probability as the long-run frequency of an event.  
If an experiment is repeated \(n\) times and event \(A\) occurs \(N_n(A)\) times, we observe the empirical frequency

$$
\frac{N_n(A)}{n}.
$$

The probability is seen as the limiting value

$$
P(A)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{N_n(A)}{n},
$$

assuming this limit exists.  
This connects directly to laws of large numbers, but it doesn’t handle unique events and depends on an idealized infinite process.

---

### 1.3 Bayesian Interpretation

The Bayesian interpretation treats probability as a measure of uncertainty or belief.  
Probabilities depend on what information an agent has at the moment.  
When new evidence \(B\) is observed, beliefs are updated by Bayes’ rule:

$$
P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B)}.
$$

This interpretation allows probabilities for one-time events, but introduces priors, which can vary from person to person.

---

### 1.4 Geometric Interpretation

In continuous settings with uniform sampling, probability is proportional to geometric size:

$$
P(A)=\frac{m(A)}{m(\Omega)},
$$

where \(m\) is length, area, or volume.  
This interpretation is the continuous analogue of the classical one.

---

### 1.5 How Kolmogorov’s Axioms Unify the Interpretations

Kolmogorov’s axioms do not favor any single interpretation.  
Instead, they provide a minimal set of rules that **all** consistent notions of probability must follow:

1) $$ P(A)\ge 0 $$  

2) $$ P(\Omega) = 1 $$

3) If $$ (A_1,A_2,\dots) $$ are disjoint, then  

$$
P\Bigl(\bigcup_i A_i\Bigr)=\sum_i P(A_i)
$$

On such:

- the classical interpretation becomes the special case of a finite uniform measure,  
- the frequentist interpretation becomes a statement about limits of empirical frequencies,  
- the Bayesian interpretation becomes a subjective measure satisfying coherence rules,  
- the geometric interpretation becomes a probability measure derived from geometric measure.

Thus all interpretations are *models* inside the same mathematical structure.

---

## 2. Probability and Measure Theory

Modern probability theory is built on measure theory.  
This allows us to handle infinite sample spaces, continuous random variables, and integration rigorously.

---

### 2.1 Sigma-Algebra

A sigma-algebra \(\mathcal{F}\) on \(\Omega\) is a collection of subsets closed under:

1. complements  
2. countable unions  
3. containing \(\Omega\) itself  

Events are the elements of \(\mathcal{F}\).  
Including only a sigma-algebra (not every possible subset) prevents contradictions and makes integration well-defined.

---

### 2.2 Probability Measure

A probability measure is a function

$$
P:\mathcal{F}\to[0,1]
$$

that satisfies Kolmogorov’s axioms.  
It assigns consistent probabilities to all events we care about.

---

### 2.3 Random Variables

A random variable is a measurable function

$$
X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}.
$$

Measurability means that for any real number \(x\):

$$
\{\omega : X(\omega)\le x\}\in\mathcal{F}.
$$

This ensures that probabilities of events involving \(X\) are well-defined.

---

### 2.4 Distribution of a Random Variable

The distribution of \(X\) is the probability measure on \(\mathbb{R}\):

$$
\mu_X(B)=P(X\in B),
$$

for any Borel set \(B\subset\mathbb{R}$.

---

### 2.5 Expectation

The expectation of \(X\) is the Lebesgue integral

$$
\mathbb{E}[X]=\int_\Omega X(\omega)\,dP(\omega),
$$

which simultaneously handles discrete, continuous, and mixed random variables.

---

## 3. Derivations from the Axioms

Below are two fundamental consequences of the probability axioms.  
They are derived **entirely** from the axioms and basic set identities.

To make the reasoning clearer, the derivations are presented in **two parallel columns**:  
- **left:** algebraic steps,  
- **right:** commentary and conclusions.

### 3.1 Subadditivity

<div class="columns">
<div class="column">

#### Steps

#### <u>Goal</u>

$$
P(A\cup B)\le P(A)+P(B).
$$

1. Decompose the union:  
   $$
   A\cup B = A\cup (B\setminus A),
   $$
   and the two sets are disjoint.

2. Apply additivity:  
   $$
   P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B\setminus A).
   $$

3. Since \(B\setminus A\subseteq B\):  
   $$
   P(B\setminus A)\le P(B).
   $$

</div>
<div class="column">

#### Explanation

- The idea is to peel off from \(B\) the part that overlaps with \(A\).  
- Additivity handles the disjoint pieces.  
- The inequality comes from monotonicity, itself a consequence of the axioms.

**Conclusion:**

$$
P(A\cup B)\le P(A)+P(B).
$$

</div>
</div>


### 3.2 Inclusion–Exclusion (Two Sets)

<div class="columns">
<div class="column">

#### Steps

<u>Goal</u>

$$
P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B).
$$

1. Split \(B\):  
   $$ B = (B\setminus A)\cup(A\cap B). $$

2. Additivity:  
   $$ P(B)=P(B\setminus A)+P(A\cap B). $$

3. Union decomposition:  
   $$ P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B\setminus A). $$

4. Substitute:  
   $$ P(B\setminus A)=P(B)-P(A\cap B). $$

</div>
<div class="column">

#### Explanation

- Corrects for double-counting.  
- The overlap \(A\cap B\) is counted twice when summing.

**Result:**  
$$ P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B). $$

</div>
</div>

function generate() {
  let lambda = parseFloat(document.getElementById("inp_lambda").value);
  let T = parseFloat(document.getElementById("inp_T").value);
  let N = parseInt(document.getElementById("inp_Nsteps").value);

  // Time grid
  let dt = T / N;
  let t = [...Array(N).keys()].map(i => i * dt);

  // Trajectory simulation
  let N_t = [];
  let count = 0;
  for (let i = 0; i < N; i++) {
    if (Math.random() < lambda * dt) count++;
    N_t.push(count);
  }

  Plotly.newPlot("plot_traj", [{
    x: t,
    y: N_t,
    mode: "lines",
    line: {shape: "hv"}
  }]);

  // Distribution
  let samples = [];
  for (let i = 0; i < N; i++) {
    let c = 0;
    for (let j = 0; j < N; j++) {
      if (Math.random() < lambda * dt) c++;
    }
    samples.push(c);
  }

  // Histogram
  Plotly.newPlot("plot_dist", [{
    x: samples,
    type: "histogram",
    histnorm: "probability"
  }]);
}
</script>
