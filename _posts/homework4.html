Law of Large Numbers

Law of Large Numbers (LLN) is a fundamental principle in probability theory that asserts that as a random experiment is repeated numerous times, the average of the outcomes will converge towards the expected value or true predicted average.

For repeated, independent trials, the empirical mean (or relative frequency) stabilizes near the theoretical expectation as the number of trials increases.

Approach

Let \(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n\) be independent and identically distributed random variables with expectation \(E[X]\). The sample mean is defined as:

$$f(n) = \frac{X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n}{n}$$

According to the Law of Large Numbers:

$$f(n) \to E[X] \quad \text{as } n \to \infty$$

Here, \(X_i\) are Bernoulli trials (success = 1, failure = 0) with success probability \(p = 0.3\). So \(E[X] = p\). As \(n\) increases, the running mean converges toward 0.3.

Interpretation

  • At the beginning of each trajectory, f(n) fluctuates randomly.
  • As the number of trials increases, fluctuations decrease and all trajectories cluster near \(p = 0.3\).
  • This illustrates the essence of the Law of Large Numbers: randomness cancels out over many repetitions.

Conclusion

  • Random results become predictable in their average.
  • Sample mean stabilizes near the true probability \(p\).
  • Variability decreases as \(n\) increases.
