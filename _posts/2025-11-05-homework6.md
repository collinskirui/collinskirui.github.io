## A. Arithmetic Mean

***Recurrence Relationships of Arithmetic Mean and Variance***: We want a formula that updates the mean each time a new data value comes in without recalculating from scratch.

Let the data points be:

$$ 
x_1, x_2,x_3,x_4,x_6,x_7,..x_n 
$$


## Arithmetic Mean with Batch Formula:

The arithmetic mean after  n samples is:

$$
\bar{x}_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i

$$

But we don’t want to store all data or recompute every time

## Derivation of Incremental (Online) Formula
We can express arithmetic mean $$ \bar{x}_n $$  in terms of previous mean $$ \bar{x}_{n-1} $$ 

$$
\bar{x}_n = \frac{1}{n} \left( \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} x_i + x_n \right)
$$

$$
= \frac{1}{n} \left( (n - 1)\bar{x}_{n-1} + x_n \right)
$$

$$
= \bar{x}_{n-1} + \frac{x_n - \bar{x}_{n-1}}{n}
$$


$$
\boxed{\bar{x}_n = \bar{x}_{n-1} + \frac{x_n - \bar{x}_{n-1}}{n}}
$$

- Xₙ - $$ \bar{x}_{n} $$ → is the difference between the new value and old mean.

- **Dividing by 𝑛** → small correction as more data accumulates.

- This makes the mean update smoothly and accurately each time.

-------
### Example (Arithmetic Mean Computation)

Given the data points:

$$
x_1 = 4, \quad x_2 = 7, \quad x_3 = 11
$$

We update the mean incrementally as follows:

1. **Initial value:**
   $$
   \bar{x}_1 = x_1 = 4
   $$

2. **Second data point:** $$ (x_2 = 7) $$
   $$
   \bar{x}_2 = \bar{x}_1 + \frac{x_2 - \bar{x}_1}{2} = 4 + \frac{7 - 4}{2} = 5.5
   $$

3. **Third data point** $$ (x_3 = 11) $$
   $$
   \bar{x}_3 = \bar{x}_2 + \frac{x_3 - \bar{x}_2}{3} = 5.5 + \frac{11 - 5.5}{3} = 7.33
   $$

Therefore, the **arithmetic mean** after three observations is:

$$
\boxed{\bar{x}_3 = 7.33}
$$

---

The **arithmetic mean** is thus:
$$
\bar{x} = \bar{x}_{n-1} + \frac{x_n - \bar{x}_{n-1}}{n}
$$

This approach fully satisfies the problem requirement to avoid **traditional batch computation**.

## B. Variance

### 1. Deriving the Recurrence for Variance
For *n* data points:

$$
x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n
$$

The total (unnormalized) sum of squared deviations is:

$$
S_n = (x_1 - \bar{x}_n)^2 + (x_2 - \bar{x}_n)^2 + \dots + (x_n - \bar{x}_n)^2
$$

where  
$$ \bar{x}_n $$ is the mean after observing *n* values.

Variance is later obtained as:

$$
\sigma_n^2 = \frac{S_n}{n} \quad \text{(population)}
$$

or

$$
s_n^2 = \frac{S_n}{n - 1} \quad \text{(sample)}
$$

Our goal: find how $$ S_n $$ can be **updated** from $$ S_{n-1} $$ when a new value $$x_n$$ arrives.

---

### 2. Express $$S_n$$ in terms of old data and the new sample $$x_n$$

We split the sum into two parts — the old data (1 to n−1) and the new one:

$$
S_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(x_i - \bar{x}_n)^2 + (x_n - \bar{x}_n)^2
$$

That means:  
**total squared deviations = old part (with new mean) + deviation of the new point.**

---

### 3. Express old deviations using the previous mean

Each old value $$x_i$$ differs from the new mean $$\bar{x}_n$$ by:

$$
x_i - \bar{x}_n = (x_i - \bar{x}_{n-1}) - (\bar{x}_n - \bar{x}_{n-1})
$$

Let the small mean change be:

$$
\Delta = \bar{x}_n - \bar{x}_{n-1}
$$

Then:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(x_i - \bar{x}_n)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n-1}[(x_i - \bar{x}_{n-1}) - \Delta]^2
$$

---

### 4. Expand the square

Expanding gives:

$$
\sum(x_i - \bar{x}_{n-1})^2 - 2\Delta \sum(x_i - \bar{x}_{n-1}) + (n - 1)\Delta^2
$$

Since by definition:

$$
\sum(x_i - \bar{x}_{n-1}) = 0
$$

the middle term vanishes, leaving:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(x_i - \bar{x}_n)^2 = S_{n-1} + (n - 1)\Delta^2
$$

---

### 5. Substitute back into the full $$ S_n $$ expression

$$
S_n = S_{n-1} + (n - 1)\Delta^2 + (x_n - \bar{x}_n)^2
$$

Now we only need to express $$\Delta$$ and $$(x_n - \bar{x}_n)$$ in simpler terms.

---

### 6. Express $$ \Delta $$ and $$ (x_n - \bar{x}_n) $$ using $$ \delta $$

Let:

$$
\delta = x_n - \bar{x}_{n-1}
$$

be the difference between the new value and the previous mean.

The new mean is updated as:

$$
\bar{x}_n = \bar{x}_{n-1} + \frac{\delta}{n}
$$

So:

$$
\Delta = \frac{\delta}{n}
$$

and also:

$$
x_n - \bar{x}_n = \delta - \Delta = \delta - \frac{\delta}{n} = \delta \times \frac{n - 1}{n}
$$

---

### 7. Substitute these into $$ S_n $$

$$
S_n = S_{n-1} + (n - 1)\left(\frac{\delta}{n}\right)^2 + \left(\delta \times \frac{n - 1}{n}\right)^2
$$

Simplify:

$$
S_n = S_{n-1} + \delta^2 \times \frac{n - 1}{n^2} \times [1 + (n - 1)]
$$

$$
S_n = S_{n-1} + \delta^2 \times \frac{n - 1}{n^2} \times n
$$

$$
S_n = S_{n-1} + \delta^2 \times \frac{n - 1}{n}
$$

---

### 8. Compact final form

Another equivalent and elegant way to write this is:

$$
S_n = S_{n-1} + \delta \times (x_n - \bar{x}_n)
$$

since:

$$
\delta \times (x_n - \bar{x}_n) = \delta^2 \times \frac{n - 1}{n}
$$

This is the **Welford recurrence formula**.

---

### 9. Conceptual interpretation

- $$ S_{n-1} $$ keeps track of all **past squared deviations**.
- The correction term $$ \delta \times (x_n - \bar{x}_n) $$ adds the **new deviation**, adjusted for the change in mean.
- **No old data needs to be reprocessed**, making this algorithm:
  - Numerically stable,
  - Memory-efficient,
  - Ideal for streaming or large datasets.

<script>
(function(){
  const input = document.getElementById('w-values');
  const btn = document.getElementById('w-btn');
  const outN = document.getElementById('w-n');
  const outMean = document.getElementById('w-mean');
  const outVar = document.getElementById('w-var');
  const err = document.getElementById('w-error');

  function parseNumbers(str) {
    if (!str) return [];
    // allow comma, space or semicolon separators
    return str.split(/[\s,;]+/)
      .map(s => s.trim())
      .filter(s => s.length>0)
      .map(Number)
      .filter(v => !Number.isNaN(v));
  }

  function compute(vals) {
    let n = 0, mean = 0, M2 = 0;
    for (let x of vals) {
      n++;
      const delta = x - mean;
      mean += delta / n;
      M2 += delta * (x - mean);
    }
    return { n, mean, M2 };
  }

  function render(vals) {
    err.style.display = 'none';
    if (vals.length === 0) {
      outN.textContent = '0';
      outMean.textContent = '—';
      outVar.textContent = '—';
      return;
    }
    const { n, mean, M2 } = compute(vals);
    outN.textContent = n;
    outMean.textContent = mean.toFixed(6);
    outVar.textContent = (n>1 ? (M2/(n-1)).toFixed(6) : 'n<2');
  }

  function onCompute() {
    const raw = input.value;
    const vals = parseNumbers(raw);
    if (raw.trim().length > 0 && vals.length === 0) {
      err.textContent = 'No valid numbers found. Use commas or spaces between numbers.';
      err.style.display = 'block';
      return;
    }
    render(vals);
  }

  // Button click
  btn.addEventListener('click', onCompute);

  // Enter key triggers compute
  input.addEventListener('keydown', function(e){
    if (e.key === 'Enter') { e.preventDefault(); onCompute(); }
  });

  // Optional: live update while typing (uncomment to enable)
  // let t; input.addEventListener('input', ()=>{ clearTimeout(t); t=setTimeout(onCompute,250); });

  // Initial render for default value
  onCompute();
})();
</script>

# Advantages of Online Algorithms

Online algorithms process data <strong>one point at a time</strong>, unlike batch algorithms that need all data at once. This gives several practical advantages.

<div style="display: flex; flex-wrap: wrap; gap: 2rem;">

<div style="flex: 1; min-width: 45%;">

<h3>Numerical Stability</h3>
<ul>
<li>Incremental updates avoid <strong>loss of precision</strong>.</li>
<li>Example: Welford’s variance is more stable than the naive batch formula:<br>
$$
\text{Var} = \frac{1}{n} \sum x_i^2 - \left(\frac{1}{n} \sum x_i \right)^2
$$
</li>
</ul>

<h3>Reduced Error</h3>
<ul>
<li>Limits <strong>catastrophic cancellation</strong> by updating differences incrementally.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Overflow/Underflow</h3>
<ul>
<li>Running totals are scaled to prevent extreme values from breaking calculations.</li>
</ul>

</div>

<div style="flex: 1; min-width: 45%;">

<h3>Computational Efficiency</h3>
<ul>
<li>Memory: Only a few variables stored (O(1)), batch requires O(n).</li>
<li>Time: Each new data point is processed in <strong>constant time</strong>.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Robustness & Scalability</h3>
<ul>
<li>Handles <strong>growing or streaming datasets</strong> naturally.</li>
<li>Scales to <strong>millions or billions of points</strong>.</li>
</ul>

### Summary

Online algorithms are superior for:

- Accurate calculations on large datasets  
- Minimizing error and catastrophic cancellation  
- Efficient memory usage  
- Real-time and streaming applications  

NB: Batch algorithms are simpler but <faint>less reliable for large or streaming datasets</faint>.
